Geopolitical Tracker Overview

Part 1 · The end goal

A monthly briefing a bank strategist can rely on

Picture a strategist at a global bank. Every month they want to know three things without spending days reading the news:

What is happening?Wars, sanctions, tariffs, elections and central-bank moves that could affect markets.
How did it change?Is each story getting worse, staying the same, calming down, or over?
Who reacted?Which governments imposed sanctions or tariffs; which central banks changed rates.

The system produces that answer automatically every 30 days: a short monthly brief, plus a database of every event, story and government action from 2018 to now. It does not predict prices. The brief is tracking, not forecasting.

Part 2 · What happens each month

Six steps, followed through February 2022

One command runs all six steps for a month: python run_cycle.py 2022-02. The grey lines show what each step produced for February 2022, the month Russia invaded Ukraine.

1

Collect the day's events

Wikipedia's "Current Events Portal" lists each day's notable world events, one line each, written by volunteer editors. The system downloads every day's list.

Feb 2022: 543 events, e.g. "Russia launches a full-scale invasion of Ukraine." Across 2018–2026: about 54,000.
2

Keep only what matters for markets

The events go in batches to Codex, an AI language model similar to ChatGPT,. It scores each one from 0 (irrelevant) to 3 (very relevant) and says why. Only scores of 2 and 3 are kept.

A sports result scores 0. The invasion scores 3: "threatens commodity flows and cross-border investment".
3

Group events into storylines

Dozens of related events become one storyline. Each storyline keeps the same ID every month so it can be followed over time. Matching this month's storylines to last month's is called linking.

259 Ukraine-related events become one storyline, "Russia-Ukraine war", continuing January's storyline.
4

Decide how each storyline moved

Codex compares last month's events with this month's and labels each storyline escalating, steady, de-escalating or resolved, with a one-line summary of what changed.

"Escalating. Tensions advanced to financial sanctions, Russian banks' SWIFT exclusion, Ukrainian capital controls, and a Russian emergency rate hike."
5

Collect government reactions

Official sources are read directly: the US Federal Register (sanctions, tariffs, export bans), the EU and UK sanctions lists, and Fed, ECB and Bank of England decisions. Each action is matched to the storyline it responds to.

US sanctions on Russian banks are linked to "Russia-Ukraine war". The EU made 518 new sanctions designations that month, the most in two years.
6

Write the brief and save the data

The most important storylines, how they moved, the reactions and the central-bank decisions go into a short report in the project's report style. Everything is also saved in a SQLite database.

File: reports/brief_2022-02.md. Point 1 is the Russia-Ukraine war; nine more storylines follow.

Side experiment A · Scenario probabilities (secondary)

For a few storylines the system also writes 3–4 possible outcomes for next month with probabilities that add up to 100%, for example "ceasefire 25%, escalation 50%, stalemate 25%". It is kept as a secondary experiment. It was weak on past data; a fair live test is scored after 2 December.

Side experiment B · Where fine-tuning fits

The main pipeline trains nothing. Codex is used as it is, by giving it written instructions.

Fine-tuning was tried only to save cost. A small model on a local graphics card (RTX 4080) was trained to copy Codex's Step 2 scores from thousands of events Codex had already scored. If it matched Codex, Step 2 would be free and instant. It came close (0.82 against Codex's 0.91 on the same test) but not close enough, so it is not used.

Part 3 · How we check the output

The ideas you need to read the numbers

There is no answer key for "what mattered this month", so the output is checked from several angles. These ideas come up in every check below.

Labels
Events where the right answer was decided in advance, so the system's answer can be marked right or wrong. Ours were written by Claude, a different AI from Codex, so they are called silver labels: useful, but not yet checked by a person.
Held-out set
Labels never looked at while building the system, so a test on them is like a real exam. The gold set was looked at while writing Codex's instructions, so its score is flattering, like studying from the exam paper. "Codex held-out 0.91" means 0.91 on the 100-event real exam.
Precision · recall · F1
Precision: of the events Codex kept, how many should have been kept? Recall: of the events that should have been kept, how many did Codex keep? F1 combines both into one score from 0 (useless) to 1 (perfect). Example: 10 events truly matter; Codex keeps 10, of which 9 are right. Precision 9/10, recall 9/10, F1 0.9.
Baseline
A deliberately simple method to compare against. Here it is a keyword count: flag any event containing words like "sanctions", "missile" or "tariff". If Codex cannot beat it, Codex is not worth using.
Correlation
How much two series move together, from −1 to +1. Around 0.5 is a clear, solid relationship; 0 is none. We mostly correlate month-to-month changes: when the reference index jumps, does the tracker jump too?
GPR index
The Geopolitical Risk index, a respected measure built by economists from how much newspapers discuss wars and tensions each month. It is our outside reference for "how much conflict was there".
Pre-registration
Writing the test and its pass mark down before running it, so the result cannot be adjusted afterwards. The older periods (2017, 2018–19, 2020–22) were tested this way, with a pass mark of 0.3.
Look-ahead
When testing on the past, accidentally using information that did not exist yet. Wikipedia pages are edited later, so every period was re-run on pages as they looked the next day.
95% CI
The range the true value very probably lies in. "0.79 (0.68–0.88)" means the real score is very likely between 0.68 and 0.88. Small samples give wide ranges.
Check 1 · Step 2

Does the filter keep the right events?

Each bar is an F1 score on Claude's labels. Higher is better; 1.0 would be perfect.

Keywords 0.40. The fine-tuned local model 0.82. Codex 0.91 on the held-out "real exam", and 0.79 when the flattering gold set is pooled in (95% CI 0.68–0.88). We report the cautious 0.79.

Check 3 · Is the AI worth it?

Codex against simple counting, period by period

The same correlation test in each period: month-to-month changes against GPR. The red dashed line is the pass mark of 0.3, written down in advance.

Codex trackerKeyword countWikipedia "Armed conflicts" countPass mark 0.3

Codex passes in every period from 2018 and fails in 2017 (0.26). In most periods the keyword count is close behind. Takeaway: for the monthly total, simple counting does nearly as well. Codex earns its place through the named, linked storylines and their movement, which counting cannot produce.

Check 4 · Is the past test fair?

No peeking at the future

Wikipedia pages get edited after the event. If those later edits were helping, results from "next-day" pages would be worse. The rule, written in advance: the difference must stay within 0.10.

The difference was +0.06 and −0.04, so later edits are not inflating results. What this cannot test is whether Codex simply remembers history from its training. Only the live test can rule that out.

Bottom line

What is shown, and what is not yet

Shown

The system reliably finds and organises the month's important developments, follows an established risk index, matches most government reactions to the right story, and does not benefit from later Wikipedia edits.

Not yet shown

That the AI beats simple keyword counting on the overall amount of conflict. Also, every check so far was marked by an AI. Human checks would give the first human-verified numbers.
